🔍 ELI5: Morse Code

  • What it is: letters turned into short "dit" and long "dah" beeps, flashes, or taps.
  • Why it was invented: Samuel Morse built it in the 1830s–40s so people could send messages instantly over telegraph wires instead of waiting days for the mail.
  • Who it was for: telegraph operators first, then sailors, soldiers, and radio operators.
  • Still used today: ham radio hobbyists, aviation beacons, and emergencies when phones and radios fail.
🎲 Random trivia: "SOS" doesn't actually stand for anything — it was picked in 1906 because ... --- ... is fast to send and impossible to mistake for something else. The very first long-distance message Morse sent in 1844 was a Bible quote: "What hath God wrought." And the letter E is just one dot because E is the most common letter in English — Morse built speed right into the alphabet.

🔍 ELI5: Semaphore

  • What it is: spelling letters by holding two flags in different positions, like a human clock.
  • Why it was invented: the Chappe brothers built an arm-signal version in 1790s France to relay messages fast, long before phones or radio existed; navies later adapted it to hand flags.
  • Who it was for: navies and armies signaling ship-to-ship or across battlefields.
  • Still used today: some navies for silent, radio-free signaling, plus Scouts, sailors, and rescue crews.
🎲 Random trivia: the peace symbol ☮ is literally semaphore — it's the letters N and D (for "nuclear disarmament") stacked on top of each other inside a circle. And the word "semaphore" is just Greek for "sign bearer." Programmers even borrowed the name for "semaphores," a tool that controls traffic between different parts of a computer program.

🔍 ELI5: Braille

  • What it is: six raised dots arranged in different patterns, one pattern per letter, read by touch.
  • Why it was invented: Louis Braille, blind since age 3, invented it in 1824 at just 15 years old to fix an older military code that was too hard to feel and read.
  • Who it was for: people who are blind or have low vision.
  • Still used today: books, elevator buttons, medicine labels, and signage worldwide.
🈶 Kind of like Chinese: plain braille spells everything letter by letter, but there's a faster "shorthand" version called Grade 2 (contracted) braille. It squishes whole common chunks — "th," "ing," "and," even entire words like "the" or "you" — into a single cell instead of spelling them out. That's the same basic idea as Chinese characters: instead of building every word sound-by-sound like an alphabet, one symbol can stand in for a whole syllable or word. Braille also isn't a language of its own — it's a code, so there's English braille, Spanish braille, Chinese braille, even a whole separate braille (Nemeth code) just for math.
🎲 Random trivia: Louis Braille invented this at 15 by shrinking a clunky 12-dot military code (meant for reading messages silently at night) down to just 6 dots — small enough to feel with one fingertip. Ironically, the school where he later taught didn't officially adopt braille until after he died.

🔍 ELI5: ASL Fingerspelling

  • What it is: spelling English letters with one hand's shapes, used inside American Sign Language.
  • Why it was invented: it grew out of French Sign Language, brought to the U.S. in 1817 by Laurent Clerc and Thomas Gallaudet to start the first American school for deaf children.
  • Who it's for: Deaf and hard-of-hearing communities across the U.S. and most of Canada.
  • Still used today: everyday conversation, spelling names and places with no sign, interpreting, and ASL classes.
🎲 Random trivia: ASL and British Sign Language aren't mutually understandable, even though the US and UK share a spoken language — ASL actually descends from French Sign Language, not English. Skilled fingerspellers can spell as fast as someone can talk, and hearing babies taught baby sign language often start "talking" with their hands months before they can speak.
